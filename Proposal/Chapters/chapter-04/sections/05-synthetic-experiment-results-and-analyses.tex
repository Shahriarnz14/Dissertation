\subsubsection{Synthetic Experiment Results and Analyses}\label{ch04:sec:synth_results}

Our analyses aim to address three questions:
\textbf{(Q1)} Which estimators perform best overall in terms of CATE RMSE?
\textbf{(Q2)} How do violations of causal assumptions (ignorability, positivity, ignorable censoring) affect performance?  
\textbf{(Q3)} How does the censoring rate influence estimation quality?  

\input{Chapters/chapter-04/figures/estimator-borda-rankings}

\textbf{Evaluation protocol.}
For each synthetic dataset, we conduct experiments with a random selection of 5,000, 2,500, and 2,500 points for training, validation, and testing samples, repeated over 10 random splits.
The validation set is used for selecting the best variant within each method family, while test sets are reserved strictly for evaluation.
Hyperparameters for each model were tuned on the validation set to minimize the Conditional Average Treatment Effect Root Mean Squared Error (CATE-RMSE).
This selection uses ground-truth effects available in the synthetic benchmark; the same criterion is unavailable in observational applications without counterfactual ground truth.
In these synthetic experiments, the horizon parameter $h$ is set to the maximum observed time in each dataset, which is a common practice that allows for consistent estimation of the RMST over the entire observed period.
Further experimental details are in Appendix~\ref{ch04:app:method_settings}; the full hyperparameter grids are in \citet[Appendix E, Tables 9--12]{noroozizadeh2026survhte}.

We present results using the following visualizations: 
\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Borda count rankings.} {To provide a clear summary across the diverse experimental settings, we adopt the Borda count method, which ranks methods by CATE RMSE in each dataset (lower is better) and then averages the ranks across datasets.
We report rankings at two levels: (i) individual estimator variants (\ChFourRebuttal{53} total; Figure~\ref{ch04:fig:average_rank_all}, top), and (ii) aggregated method families, where the best variant per family is selected on validation data (\ChFourRebuttal{11} total; Figure~\ref{ch04:fig:average_rank_all}, bottom).}

    \item \textbf{CATE RMSE.} We report absolute CATE RMSE across 10 repeats, grouped by survival scenario, with one panel per set of eight causal configurations.
Here, we show Scenario \texttt{C} as an illustrative example (Figure~\ref{ch04:fig:cate_mse_scenario_C_main}); results for the other scenarios are deferred to \citet[Appendix F.4, Figures 8--12]{noroozizadeh2026survhte}.

    \item \textbf{ATE bias.} We report ATE bias results for Scenario \texttt{D} in Appendix~\ref{ch04:app:more-ate-plots}.
While the focus of this benchmark is on CATE estimation, these serve as a complementary check.

\end{itemize}

Rankings summarize relative performance; CATE RMSE measures error in the estimated conditional effects, while ATE bias measures error in the population average.

\textbf{Overall performance (Q1).}

Overall, performance is strongly context-dependent.

At the method family level (Figure~\ref{ch04:fig:average_rank_all}, bottom), S-Learner-Survival (average rank \ChFourRebuttal{3.30 across 11 method families}) and Matching-Survival (3.48) maintain their advantage, followed by Double-ML (3.98) and Causal Survival Forests (5.10).

\textbf{Violations of causal assumptions (Q2).}

Performance shifts substantially depending on assumption violations \citep[Appendix F.3, Figure 7]{noroozizadeh2026survhte}.
In randomized balanced trials (\texttt{RCT-50}), outcome imputation methods remain competitive, with Double-ML (3.60) and Causal Forest (5.60) showing performance comparable to high-ranking survival meta-learners.
However, under imbalanced treatment (\texttt{RCT-5}), Double-ML remains strong (1.80), while T-Learner-Survival---which relies on fitting base models on treated units---drops to last place (9.00), alongside SurvITE, due to sparsity of treated samples.

Under positivity violation (\texttt{OBS-NoPos}, \citep[Figure 7e]{noroozizadeh2026survhte}), we see the more sophisticated outcome imputation approaches like Double-ML and X-Learner maintain strong performance and outperform survival meta-learners.
However, when positivity violations occur alongside other violations \citep[Figure 7h]{noroozizadeh2026survhte}, survival meta-learners regain their advantage, demonstrating their robustness to multiple simultaneous violations.

Under informative censoring (\texttt{InfC}, Figure~\ref{ch04:fig:cate_rmse_scenario_C_OBS_CPS_InfC}-~\ref{ch04:fig:cate_rmse_scenario_C_OBS_NoPos_InfC}), performance and variability differ across methods and causal configurations.
Several methods exhibit greater variability than in the corresponding ordinary-censoring settings, these comparisons change both the censoring mechanism and the realized censoring rate (Table~\ref{ch04:tab:synthetic-data-censor-rate}).

\textbf{Impact of censoring rate (Q3).}

We observe apparent divergence of the estimated ATE from the true ATE in Scenario \texttt{D} (Figure~\ref{ch04:fig:ate_bias_grid_scenario_D}).

The ordinary configurations in this scenario have high censoring, whereas the \texttt{-InfC} configurations have lower realized censoring rates (Table~\ref{ch04:tab:synthetic-data-censor-rate}).

The semi-synthetic censoring sweep in Section~\ref{ch04:sec:semi-syn-data-experiment} further examines sensitivity to censoring with fixed covariate and event-time mechanisms.

Full rankings, win-rate summaries, auxiliary-model evaluations, and convergence analyses are provided in \citet[Appendices F.1--F.3 and F.6--F.7]{noroozizadeh2026survhte}.
